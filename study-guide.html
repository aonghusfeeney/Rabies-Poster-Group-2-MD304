Rabies: a revision guide

The short version. Rabies is one topic among many on the neglected tropical diseases material, so this covers what is worth knowing and stops there.

Companion to the e-poster 100% Preventable, 100% Fatal: Barriers to rabies elimination on the road to “Zero by 30” · School of Medicine, University of Galway

How to use this

Read the notes once, then work through Test yourself. The questions are the point — recall beats re-reading. The numbers section is the only part worth memorising.

Context

What makes a disease “neglected”

The WHO list of neglected tropical diseases groups around twenty conditions that share a profile rather than a pathogen. They affect poor, rural and politically marginal populations; they cause chronic disability or death rather than epidemics that force a response; and they attract disproportionately little research funding and political attention.

“Neglected” describes our attention, not the organism. That is the examinable idea, and rabies is the clearest case on the list — because the tools to prevent every single death have existed for over a century.

The disease

Rabies in brief

  • Cause. A lyssavirus, almost always transmitted in the saliva of an infected animal through a bite or scratch. Dogs account for up to 99% of human cases.
  • Pathogenesis. The virus travels from the wound along peripheral nerves to the central nervous system. It does not travel in the blood, which is why there is no useful diagnostic test during the incubation period.
  • Presentation. Acute progressive encephalomyelitis. Classically fever, then altered consciousness, then autonomic instability, hydrophobia and aerophobia; a paralytic form also occurs and is more often misdiagnosed.
  • Outcome. Once clinical signs appear rabies is almost invariably fatal, with death within days. There is no effective treatment; care is palliative.
  • Distribution. An estimated 59,000 deaths a year, more than 95% of them in Africa and Asia.

The key concept

The incubation period is the whole argument

Incubation is typically two to three months, exceeding a year in 2-3% of cases. Throughout that time the person has no symptoms. This is the one exploitable weakness in a disease that is otherwise always fatal.

Post-exposure prophylaxis given properly and in time prevents essentially every death. Everything that matters about rabies happens inside that window.

Post-exposure prophylaxis has three components

  • Wound washing with soap and water — immediate, and the step most often skipped
  • Rabies vaccine, as a course over days
  • Rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated into and around the wound, for the most severe exposures only

WHO exposure categories decide what is given

CategoryExposureAction
ITouching or feeding an animal; licks on intact skinWash. No prophylaxis
IINibbling of uncovered skin; minor scratches without bleedingWash and vaccinate
IIITransdermal bites or scratches; licks on broken skin; mucous-membrane contamination; any bat contactWash, vaccinate and give immunoglobulin

Source: WHO Expert Consultation on Rabies, Technical Report Series 1012. Worth knowing that bat exposure is category III regardless of how trivial the contact looks.

Regimens are getting shorter

Intradermal administration uses a fraction of a vial per patient instead of a whole one, so the same vaccine supply treats far more people. The WHO-recommended one-week intradermal regimen requires only three clinic visits, against four or five for older intramuscular schedules.

The global health argument

Why a preventable disease still kills

This is our poster's angle, and it is the part most likely to be useful in an exam answer, because it converts rabies from a microbiology question into a health-systems one. Three failures:

1. We cannot count it

Two current estimates of the same quantity do not overlap. The modelled figure is 59,000 deaths a year (95% CI 25,000-159,200); the Global Burden of Disease study gives 10,181 cases for 2021 (95% UI 6,080-14,293). No single value is consistent with both.

Meanwhile 2,301 deaths were reported to WHO for 2024, from 79 of 194 member states. If you cannot measure a disease you cannot prioritise it, budget for it, or ever demonstrate elimination.

2. People cannot reach prophylaxis

In a nationwide Indian survey of dog-bite victims, 83.6% reached a health facility, 79.5% received at least one vaccine dose, 66.2% received at least three — and 40.5% completed the course. Four in five start; two in five finish.

The most commonly reported barrier across 20 countries of the WHO African Region was not cost but not knowing rabies was treatable (16 of 20), then no facility within reach (14) and use of traditional medicine (13). Cost ranked sixth of eight — which matters, because awareness is cheaper to fix than subsidy.

Immunoglobulin reached 61 of 546 Indian patients with severe wounds (11.2%), and only 5 of 20 African countries have any immunoglobulin product licensed or in use.

3. We fund the consequence, not the cause

Rabies costs about US$8.6 billion a year. Premature death accounts for 55.2%, prophylaxis 20.1%, income lost seeking prophylaxis 15.5%, livestock losses 6.0% — roughly 98% of the burden falls after transmission has already happened.

Dog vaccination, the only intervention that interrupts transmission, receives 1.5%. Laboratory surveillance receives 0.009%. Latin America is the exception at 17%, and dog-mediated rabies has since been eliminated from several Latin American countries.

The single finding that ties the three together: of 89 laboratory-confirmed rabies deaths in people who had received prophylaxis, only three were true breakthrough infections. The rest had delayed initiation, undocumented wound care, missing immunoglobulin or incomplete schedules.

The vaccine is not failing. The delivery is.

Local relevance

Ireland is rabies-free, not risk-free

  • No terrestrial rabies since 1903, but classified low risk rather than no risk, because European bat lyssaviruses circulate across Europe and all Irish bats are considered potential hosts.
  • 150-200 potential exposures are assessed in Ireland each year. More than 95% follow exposure to an animal abroad, mainly dogs.
  • Around 80% of those presenting have already started prophylaxis overseas and attend to complete the course — so Irish practice sits downstream of prophylaxis systems in endemic countries.

A useful exam point: a rabies-free country still needs a functioning exposure assessment pathway, because risk is imported rather than absent.

Policy

Zero by 30

In 2018 WHO, FAO, WOAH (then OIE) and the Global Alliance for Rabies Control committed, under the United Against Rabies partnership, to zero human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by 2030. Note the precise wording — not zero rabies, and dog-mediated only.

WHO's own current assessment is that the goal is “still achievable in some countries”. Three countries have been validated for elimination of dog-mediated human rabies: Bhutan, Chile and Mexico.

Modelling across 67 rabies-endemic countries found that expanding free prophylaxis with the one-week intradermal regimen would avert an additional 494,700 deaths by 2035 while total projected expenditure fell by US$32.5 million — because intradermal dosing splits a vial, so 17.4 million more people are vaccinated on the same ~73 million vials.

Recall practice

Test yourself

Answer out loud before tapping. Twelve questions.

1. Why is the incubation period clinically important?

It is the only window in which the disease can be stopped. The virus travels along peripheral nerves to the CNS over weeks to months, during which the person is asymptomatic and prophylaxis is still effective. Once it reaches the CNS and symptoms begin, rabies is almost invariably fatal.

2. Name the three components of post-exposure prophylaxis.

Wound washing with soap and water; rabies vaccine; and rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated into and around the wound for category III exposures.

3. A patient is licked on intact skin by a stray dog. What category, and what do you give?

Category I. Wash the area; no prophylaxis indicated.

4. A patient has a minor scratch from a bat, no bleeding. What category?

Category III — any bat contact is category III regardless of how minor it appears. Wash, vaccinate and give immunoglobulin. The trap here is grading it as II on the basis of the wound alone.

5. Why does intradermal administration matter so much?

It uses a fraction of a vial per patient rather than a whole one, so the same vaccine supply treats far more people — in one model, 17.4 million more people on the same ~73 million vials. It also shortens the course to three visits. The efficiency gain requires no new product, which is why non-adoption is a delivery failure rather than a technological one.

6. What does it mean that two burden estimates “do not overlap”?

The modelled estimate is 59,000 deaths (95% CI 25,000-159,200); the Global Burden of Disease figure is 10,181 cases (95% UI 6,080-14,293). The intervals share no common value, so no single true figure is consistent with both. The disagreement is itself the finding.

7. Why can't rabies deaths simply be counted?

Deaths occur within about a week, mostly in rural areas, often at home and outside any health system, and there are no survivors to count backwards from. There is no chronic phase and no case register. Surveillance captures a fraction: 2,301 deaths reported to WHO for 2024, from 79 of 194 member states.

8. What was the most commonly reported barrier to prophylaxis in the WHO African Region, and why is that surprising?

Not knowing rabies was treatable — reported by 16 of 20 countries. Cost ranked sixth of eight. It is surprising because a course can run to 31 days' wages in Asia and 51 in Africa, so cost is severe where it applies. But it is not what ministries name first, and that changes what you would fund: awareness is cheaper than subsidy.

9. If prophylaxis is nearly 100% effective, why do people still die after receiving it?

Almost always because of delivery failure rather than vaccine failure. Of 89 laboratory-confirmed deaths in people who had received prophylaxis, only three were true breakthrough infections; the rest had delayed initiation, undocumented wound care, missing immunoglobulin or incomplete schedules.

10. Why is spending on dog vaccination the central economic argument?

It is the only intervention that interrupts transmission, and it receives about 1.5% of the US$8.6 billion annual cost, while roughly 98% of that cost falls after transmission has already occurred. Vaccinating the dog protects everyone it would have bitten; treating the person saves one life and leaves the dog circulating.

11. Why does a rabies-free country still need a rabies pathway?

Because risk is imported rather than absent. Ireland has had no terrestrial rabies since 1903 but is classified low risk rather than no risk, because bat lyssaviruses circulate in Europe. 150-200 exposures are assessed annually, more than 95% acquired abroad, and around 80% of those presenting are completing a course started overseas.

12. State the Zero by 30 target precisely.

Zero human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by 2030 — set in 2018 by WHO, FAO, WOAH and the Global Alliance for Rabies Control under United Against Rabies. Not zero rabies, and not all reservoirs. WHO currently describes it as “still achievable in some countries”.
