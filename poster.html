Barriers to rabies elimination on the road to “Zero by 30”

Abstract

Background

Rabies is a viral zoonosis that is almost universally fatal once symptoms appear, yet is entirely preventable by vaccination. An estimated 59,000 people die each year, over 95% of them in Africa and Asia. The WHO, WOAH, FAO and partners aim to end human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by 2030.

Aims

To examine why a preventable disease continues to kill, and whether the “Zero by 30” target remains attainable.

Methods

Structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Embase, supplemented by guidance from WHO, WOAH, Gavi and national agencies.

Findings

Three failures sustain the burden. Surveillance detects a fraction of deaths, so the burden cannot be measured. Prophylaxis exists but is not reached; in India only 40.5% of dog-bite victims complete treatment. And just 1.5% of the disease's US$8.6 billion annual cost is spent on dog vaccination, the only intervention that halts transmission.

Conclusion

Rabies persists as a failure of delivery, not of technology.

Background

Rabies is an acute progressive encephalomyelitis caused by lyssaviruses. Once clinical signs develop it is almost invariably fatal, with death following within days.1 Dogs account for up to 99% of human cases.2 In endemic countries, children are four times as likely as adults to develop rabies — they are more likely to be exposed and less likely to report it.3

The disease's one exploitable weakness is its incubation period: two to three months in most cases, exceeding a year in 2-3%.1 This window makes post-exposure prophylaxis possible, and current prophylaxis already prevents an estimated 56,000 deaths each year.4

Rabies is not a disease awaiting a scientific breakthrough. The vaccines, the immunoglobulins and the veterinary tools all exist. This poster examines why, despite that, an estimated 59,000 people continue to die annually and what needs to change to reach zero human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by 2030.2

Methods

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Embase on 17 September 2026, combining the terms rabies, post-exposure prophylaxis, elimination, One Health, burden and access using Boolean operators and database-specific indexing (MeSH, Emtree).

Searches were restricted to English-language publications from 2015 onwards (2018 onwards for prophylaxis-access questions, to reflect the revised WHO regimens). Evidence was supplemented by hand-searched guidance from WHO, WOAH, Gavi, CDC, NIAC and the HPSC.

Failure 1 — we cannot count it

Two estimates of the same burden do not overlap

The discrepancy should run the other way: the Global Burden of Disease codes rabies of any origin, the 59,000 figure only dog-mediated infection.6 The broader definition returns the smaller number.

Fewer than half of Member States submitted any rabies data for 2024, and a third of the African Region submitted none.2 Rabies is almost invariably fatal within days of symptom onset,1 so it leaves no survivors to press its case.

The same pattern, at national level

20,847 3.6× 5,726

Modelled for India5 — 35% of the global estimate — against a nationwide household survey of 337,808 people (95% UI 3,967-7,350).7

If India's survey is right, what is the global total?

The 2015 model attributes 35% of the world's rabies deaths to India.5 India's own 2022-23 household survey measured far fewer than the model assumes.7 Hold India's share at 35% and drag the measured figure to see what global total it implies.

Without a credible baseline, progress towards zero cannot be measured — and elimination cannot be demonstrated.

Failure 2 — people cannot reach it

Four in five start. Two in five finish.

1,576 people, one dot each

Every dot is one person in the Indian survey who reported a dog bite.7 Watch where they are lost — each band is sized to the number of people in it.

1,576 people bitten by a dog.

638 completed the course 615 started and stopped 323 never started

Run the cascade on any number of bites

Apply the Indian survey's observed rates7 to any number of people bitten, and see how many reach the end of a course.

people bitten

Received ≥1 vaccine dose 79.5%
Received ≥3 doses 66.2%
Completed the course 40.5%
Started but never finished 49.1% of those who started

What this assumes. That the rates measured in one nationwide Indian survey, 2022-23, hold for whatever population you enter. They will not hold everywhere — the point is the shape of the loss, not a forecast. On the 7.7 million preset: the survey reports 9.1 million animal bites a year in India, at 6.6 per 1,000 population, and a separate dog-bite incidence of 5.6 per 1,000.7 Because every rate in this cascade was measured among dog-bite victims specifically, the preset scales 9.1 million by 5.6/6.6 to give roughly 7.7 million dog bites. On the paper's dog-bite confidence interval of 4.8-6.6 per 1,000 that range is about 6.6-9.1 million.

The price of a course

US$18 20× US$365

Public-facility cost of a full course, Côte d'Ivoire to Ghana. It is free in only 10 of 20 countries2; where charged, a course costs 31 days' wages in Asia and 51 in Africa.1

The biologics do not arrive

  • 11.2%of Indian bite victims with laceration, puncture or avulsion wounds received immunoglobulin7
  • 5 of 20African countries where any immunoglobulin product is licensed or in use2
  • 14 of 18reported vaccine or immunoglobulin shortages within two years2
  • 4 of 17use the dose-sparing one-week intradermal regimen2

Not one of these is a failure of the vaccine. Every one is a failure of the system meant to deliver it.8

Failure 3 — we fund the consequence, not the cause

Only one intervention stops transmission. It gets 1.5% of the money.

Spending more would cost less

494,700 -US$66 each -US$32.5m

Additional deaths averted across 67 countries by 2035 by expanding free prophylaxis with the one-week intradermal regimen, incremental to the status quo — while total expenditure falls, at an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of -US$66 per death averted.4

What coordination would buy

  • US$4.47average cost per dog vaccinated across a coordinated African programme9
  • US$9.5bnprojected welfare gain 2024-2054 (95% CI 8.1-11.4), if every country cooperates9
  • 17.4mmore people vaccinated on the same ~73 million vials, because intradermal dosing splits a vial4

The obstacle is not the price of the intervention. It is that nobody has budgeted for it.

What is changing

The tools are improving faster than the systems delivering them

Monoclonal antibodies are displacing plasma-derived immunoglobulin, which is in chronically short supply — though of the 20 African Region countries surveyed, only Nigeria and Togo reported using a monoclonal product.2 A phase 4 trial randomised 3,994 patients with category III exposures and found a monoclonal antibody regimen immunogenically comparable to equine immunoglobulin (day-14 geometric mean concentration 16.05 vs 13.48 IU/mL), with no participant developing rabies during one year of follow-up.10 Sixty participants had to be withdrawn from the equine immunoglobulin arm after a positive skin test, and three developed urticaria causally related to equine immunoglobulin despite a negative test.10 Monoclonals are manufactured recombinantly, so supply is scalable in a way that donor plasma is not.

Integrated bite case management links animal investigation to human treatment decisions, targeting prophylaxis at genuine exposures. Modelling suggests it reduces vaccine requirements below 21.3 million vials while improving cost-effectiveness as elimination approaches.4

Financing has become less certain, not more. Gavi announced expanded access to human rabies vaccines for prophylaxis in June 2024; by 2026 rabies had been moved into Gavi's discretionary fund, flexible funding that countries allocate across eligible programmes. Only four countries in the WHO African Region applied before the change.2

Ireland and the traveller

A rabies-free country is not a risk-free country

Ireland has been free of terrestrial rabies since 1902, but is classified as low risk, not no risk. European bat lyssaviruses circulate across Europe, and all Irish bats are considered potential rabies hosts.3

Approximately 150-200 potential rabies exposures are assessed in Ireland annually. More than 95% follow exposure to a terrestrial animal abroad, mainly dogs. Around 80% of those presenting have already started PEP overseas and attend for series completion.3

Global elimination is not a distant concern. Irish clinical practice is downstream of prophylaxis systems in endemic countries.

Limitations

  • Global burden estimates are largely modelled, relying on assumptions about bite incidence and health-seeking behaviour that vary substantially between settings.
  • Cost-effectiveness comparisons are not directly comparable. Human PEP is typically evaluated over a 1-year period, while dog vaccination programmes are assessed over 4-10 years.
  • English-language restriction may have excluded relevant regional evidence, particularly from West Africa and Latin America.
  • WHO's African Region survey drew on 20 of 47 Member States, self-reported by ministries of health, and may not be representative.

Recommendations for further research

  • Reconciliation of burden estimates. The six-fold modelled-Global Burden discrepancy and the national-level inconsistencies in India remain unresolved. Progress towards zero cannot be measured without a credible baseline.
  • Evaluate monoclonal antibodies at scale: immunogenicity comparable to equine immunoglobulin,10 but field effectiveness and cost where immunoglobulin is unavailable remain untested.
  • Prospective evaluation of single-visit and routine childhood pre-exposure prophylaxis in endemic populations. Current evidence is largely from travellers or from modelling.
  • Broaden economic evaluations to incorporate livestock losses and animal welfare, often omitted, which may lead to the benefits of dog vaccination being underestimated.

Conclusions

  • Rabies remains almost universally fatal yet almost entirely preventable. The tools required to eliminate it — vaccines, immunoglobulins, monoclonal antibodies and veterinary programmes — already exist.
  • The disease persists because of three linked delivery failures: surveillance that cannot reliably measure the burden, prophylaxis that patients cannot access or complete, and financing focused on consequences of transmission rather than its source.
  • Improving access to PEP is cost-saving, and coordinated dog vaccination can generate substantial net economic benefit.
  • WHO judges the 2030 target still achievable in some countries.
  • Rabies is therefore best understood not as a neglected tropical disease awaiting innovation, but as a test of whether existing tools can be delivered to the people who need them.
